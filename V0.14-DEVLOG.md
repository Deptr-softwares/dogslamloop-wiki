### 1. Per-character discussion threads

- [x] Schema: threads keyed by `page_id`, one thread per character page, with posts under it
- [x] RLS: anyone signed in posts; `viewer` (the soft ban) cannot; staff can hide or remove *(`can_moderate` lands with §2)*
- [x] Thread renders on the character page, below the tabs
- [x] Rate limiting on posting, reusing the existing `check_revision_rate_limit` shape rather than a second mechanism
- [x] Escape at every interpolation — post bodies and author names are attacker-reachable
- [x] Empty state that reads as an invitation, not a broken section

#### Shipped 2026-08-13

**One table, not two.** A `threads` table would hold nothing but a `page_id`
every post already carries — a join to learn something the child row states.
The thread is the page.

**Owner decisions, taken before the schema rather than after:** one level of
replies (a reply aimed at a reply is *flattened* to the same list rather than
rejected — the reader's intent is fine, only the depth is not); separate rate
limits; delete-only.

**Delete-only is why there is no author `UPDATE` policy at all.** RLS says
which *rows* may change, never which *columns* — so any policy letting an
author touch their own row would also let them rewrite it after three people
had replied. Removal goes through `remove_my_discussion_post`, a
`SECURITY DEFINER` RPC that can only blank the body and set the status.

**The body is blanked, not hidden.** Same reason: a removed post has to stay
readable so replies under it aren't orphans, and row-level security cannot hide
one column from a reader allowed to see the row.

Four things found while building, none of them in the plan:

- **`user_notifications` has a staff-only INSERT policy.** Correct for its
  original purpose, and it means one contributor cannot write a notification to
  another — which is exactly what a reply is. Reply notifications had to become
  a `SECURITY DEFINER` trigger.
- **`anonymize_user_by_email` hard-deletes the `auth.users` row.** A plain
  foreign key here would have made deleting anyone who had ever posted fail —
  the identical trap `pending_revisions.author_id` already documents. Fixed by
  `ON DELETE SET NULL` plus an explicit re-attribution step, so posts survive
  their author the way revisions do.
- **`author_name` would have shown the wrong name.** `getDisplayName` prefers a
  custom profile name, then the OAuth full name, then the Discord `global_name`
  claim, and only then the email prefix. Deriving it from the address in SQL
  would have printed a different name in threads than the same person shows
  everywhere else — which reads as two accounts. The trigger now mirrors that
  priority order exactly.
- **Authorship is set server-side and the client never sends it.** A revision
  can take `author_name` from the submitting page because a human reviews it
  before anyone sees it. A post is public immediately, so a client-supplied
  name would be one-line impersonation of any staff member.

`tests/discussions.spec.js`, 14 tests. Deliberately scoped to what a browser
can prove — rendering, escaping, who is offered a composer, and that the client
claims no authorship. RLS, the rate-limit trigger, the flattening and the
notification trigger are unreachable from Playwright and are probed live after
merge.

- [x] Moderation actions are logged, not silent — a removal nobody can audit is indistinguishable from data loss

#### Shipped 2026-08-13

**Threads shipped with staff holding a direct `UPDATE` grant, and that is now
dropped.** A reviewer with direct UPDATE can rewrite a contributor's words —
exactly the power the delete-only decision withheld from the *author*.
Withholding it from the author and handing it to staff is the wrong way round.
Every moderation action now goes through one RPC that can only set a status,
blank a body and write a log row. **There is no path in the schema by which
anybody edits somebody else's post text.**

**Two verbs, because they are genuinely different operations:**

| | Hide | Remove |
|---|---|---|
| Row | filtered out of the public result set | stays, as a placeholder |
| Body | kept | blanked |
| Reversible | yes | yes, from the log snapshot |
| For | might be a problem | definitely is |

**Hiding works as a row-level rule where item 1 could not use one.** Removing a
*row* from a result set is what RLS does; hiding a *column* from a reader
allowed to see the row is not expressible — which is why remove blanks the body
rather than trusting a policy. The two verbs fall out of that constraint rather
than being a UX preference.

**A reason is required for hide and remove, enforced in the RPC.** The log
exists so a removal can be audited, and an entry saying only that somebody
removed something is the same silence with a timestamp on it. Restore is
exempt — putting something back needs no justification.

**Restore pulls the body back out of the log**, which is the only copy after a
remove. Without it, remove would be irreversible in practice even though the
status says otherwise. Staff deliberately *cannot* restore a `removed_by_author`
post: putting someone's words back after they withdrew them is not moderation.

Also in this PR, at the owner's request: **a jump-to-discussion button on the
state-chip row.** It is a *sibling* of `#character-mode-bar`, not a child —
that bar hides itself for a character with fewer than two states, which is 20
of the 22, so a button inside it would have existed on two pages. It carries
the post count, and shows nothing rather than `0` when the thread is empty,
because an explicit zero reads as a dead feature.

Two bugs found by the tests rather than by reasoning:

- `insertBefore(badge, body)` where `body` was not yet a child of `wrap` —
  throws, taking the whole thread render down with it.
- The required-reason field's **native** validation swallowed the submit event
  before the handler ran, so the message appeared in a browser bubble while
  every other message in the file appears in the status line. `form.noValidate`
  plus a hand-rolled check, so there is one error channel.

`tests/discussions.spec.js` is now 23 tests. `tests/user-capabilities.spec.js`
grew to 9: its `.personnel-capability-box` selector had become ambiguous with

#### Shipped 2026-08-13 — the editor

`tier-editor.html`, its own page rather than a branch inside `edit.html`, and
the reason is the permission model rather than convenience. `edit.html` is the
contributor editor: everything written there becomes a pending revision a
reviewer approves. A tier list is **not reviewed** — it belongs to one named
person who publishes directly — so routing it through that page would mean a
second workflow living inside a file whose entire shape is the first one. The
two-pane layout, the block editor and the button families are all reused.

**The note rule is implemented as a diff, not a field.** The editor snapshots
where everyone started, and every difference becomes a row that has to be
explained before SAVE does anything. A changelog written afterwards is written
from memory; the notes worth having are the ones somebody wrote while they
still knew why.

Four consequences that fell out of doing it that way, each of which would have
been a bug if it had been a plain form:

- **Moving a character back where it started cancels the move**, and discards
  the half-typed note with it.
- **Renaming a tier is not a move.** The diff keys on tier name, so a rename
  would otherwise register as everyone in that tier moving — both wrong and a
  wall of notes to write. Renaming rewrites the baseline instead.
- **Removing a tier returns its occupants to unranked as explainable moves**,
  because removing a tier is a ranking decision about everybody in it.
- **A failed save keeps the moves and their notes.** Re-baselining before the
  write would silently discard the very moves it failed to record.

The unranked tray is **recomputed from the roster rather than stored**, so a
character added to the wiki after a list was written appears on the next edit
instead of being invisible forever. Boomcat is exactly that case today.

Drag-and-drop is written fresh rather than reused from `js/tierlist.js`: that
file's editor operates on the old `desc_data.tabs` model and is the
least-audited major file in the repo, so binding a new feature to it would mean
inheriting both its data model and its bugs. Pointer events, so mouse and touch
are one implementation. `window.tierEditorPlace` is the operation the drag
triggers, which also makes it drivable by tests without synthesising pointer
gestures.

`tests/tier-editor.spec.js`, 15 tests.
- [ ] **A tier list per assigned individual.** The owner assigns people; each keeps their own list, with their own reasoning and changelog, maintained as the game updates. From a discussion about objective versus subjective meta

**Note on the versioning item:** this is a data reshape of live content, so it needs the same treatment the matchup tier rename got — a migration, a renderer that reads both shapes during the transition, and `page_history` left alone. `js/tierlist.js` is also the least-audited major file in the repo, and its live-preview is intentionally disabled.

Split from the editor deliberately: this half is the data model and the public
page, and it ships working with the owner's list already in it.

**Nothing was deleted.** `page_data.desc_data` for `tierlist` still holds all

**Why a table rather than more JSON, and it is not tidiness.**
`desc_data.tabs` held one current state per tab and no history, so "what
changed, when, and why" had nowhere to live. Per-person ownership makes that
decisive: **RLS cannot say "this person may edit their own entry inside this
JSON blob"**, only "this person may edit this row". The permission model forces
the shape.

**The character keys were rewritten**, with the owner's go-ahead. The old tabs
stored navigation display slugs (`Sus-Sister`, `Mangaka`, `Locust`) and leaned
on a normalizer that lowercased and stripped punctuation. Checked against live
data: all 21 matched — but three of them (`Locust`→`locust_guy`,
`Mangaka`→`aspiring_mangaka`, `Sus-Sister`→`crow_charmer`) differ from their
real `page_id` by more than punctuation and matched only because the display
slug happened to agree, which is not a property anyone was maintaining. Now
stored as `page_id`, the key every other table already uses. The migration maps
them by joining `site_pages` on a normalized `nav_id`, and **keeps an unmatched
key rather than dropping the placement** — losing one silently is worse than
showing one that needs fixing.

Also found while checking: **Boomcat is in the roster and in no tier**, having
been added after the list was last touched.

**The note-per-move rule is in the schema**, not only the editor: `note` is
`NOT NULL` with a length floor, and a change row whose `from_tier` equals its
`to_tier` is rejected outright. A changelog reading "moved Vessel from D to B"
and nothing else is exactly the artefact the rule exists to prevent, so the
editor cannot be the only thing standing in its way.

Two fine-tuning items from §8 came along, since this rebuilt the markup they
applied to: **bigger character icons** (60px → 78px) and **tighter spacing**

`tests/certified-tier-lists.spec.js`, 13 tests. One caught a wrong assumption

**A retired class name nearly came back.** The new stylesheet used `.tier-row`

The test was right and the code was wrong: every new class is now namespaced

Four tests in that spec covered the old renderer *through the tier list page*,

**What this simplification buys.** The earlier concern was 22 tabs multiplied by N people — 110 lists at five assignees, most of which nobody would maintain. It is now N lists, and lazy loading falls out for free: one list is fetched when its name is clicked, rather than 22 tabs of placements on page load.

#### The permission model is still the new part

Attributing a ranking to a person is the right instinct for a technical wiki. Frame data is measurable and a tier list is opinion, and a single unattributed ranking quietly presents the second as the first — *"the wiki says this character is S tier"* is an argument nobody can win. A named list is a claim someone is accountable for, which is both more honest and less brigade-able than an official-looking consensus.

For reference, the structure being replaced, measured against production before the decision:

```
desc_data.tabs → 22 tabs
   overall            6 tiers,  1 changelog entry
   vs-honored-one     5 tiers,  1 changelog entry
   ... 20 more "vs <character>" tabs
tier      = { name, color, characters }
changelog = { date, notes }
```

**The permission model is new to this codebase.** Page access today is role-based — `page_permissions.required_role` gates a whole page, and anyone holding the role edits all of it. This needs per-row ownership: the assignee edits *their* list and not each other's, with admins above all of it. That is closer to the capability work than to the role work, and it is the part most likely to be underestimated.

### 4. Free Submit Tier List, with anti-brigading built in

- [ ] Schema: one row per `(user_id, character)` vote, with `UNIQUE(user_id, character)` so a resubmission updates rather than stacks
- [ ] Aggregate to a ranking, with the **sample size stored and displayed**
- [ ] Account-age or contribution gate on voting
- [ ] Rate limiting on submission
- [ ] Show the **distribution**, not only the headline number
- [ ] **Median, not mean** — owner's decision, 2026-08-13. Decided before the schema exists rather than after, because changing it later means recomputing every published ranking
- [ ] The tool page exists and is empty: `tools/free-submit-tier-list/`, category `Community`

**What choosing the median commits to.** A median needs every individual vote kept, not a running total — you cannot compute one from a sum and a count the way you can a mean. So votes are stored per person and the ranking is derived, which is what the `UNIQUE(user_id, character)` row already implies. It also makes the distribution free: once the individual votes are there, showing the spread costs a query rather than a schema change.

The payoff is specific. A block of coordinated S-tier votes drags a mean immediately, while a median does not move until that block is more than half of everyone who voted. Against a Discord that can produce a few hundred votes in an afternoon, that is the difference between a ranking that reports the loudest group and one that reports the community.

### 5. Deleting media from the queue

**Owner's design, 2026-08-13**, and it is simpler than the quarantine-prefix sketch it replaces: deletion is a deliberate per-item action taken in the media queue, not an automatic consequence of flagging. Flagging still hides; deleting is what makes a file unreachable, and a person decides.

- [x] A delete button per item in the media queue, removing the storage object
- [x] Gated by **admin OR `can_delete_media`** — the third capability, following the v0.13 pattern
- [x] Whitelist `can_delete_media` in `set_user_capability`, which rejects unknown names by design
- [x] Confirmation naming the file, and saying plainly that it cannot be undone
- [x] Deleting must also settle the moderation row, so the queue does not keep listing a file that is gone
- [x] Warn when deleting something still referenced by a live page — the usage data is already there, and this is the one place it stops being advisory

#### Shipped 2026-08-13

**`can_delete_media` is deliberately narrower than `can_moderate`.** Moderation
comes free with reviewer; this does not, and falls back only to admin.
Reviewing a revision and destroying a file are different amounts of trust, and
this is the only irreversible action on the site.

**The confirmation is inline rather than a prompt**, because it has to show the
file name *and* its usage together, which a one-line text prompt cannot. Three
different warnings depending on what the usage data says:

| Usage | What it says |
|---|---|
| On live pages | names them, and that deleting breaks them |
| History/pending only | restoring one later would restore a broken reference |
| Nothing found | **"that is not the same as nothing using it"** |

That third wording is the important one. `media_usage()` finds references by
*extraction*, which can miss an unusual form; the garbage collector answers the
same question by conservative substring matching precisely so its mistakes fall
towards keeping files alive. "Nothing references it" has always meant "nothing
was found", and the person about to destroy a file is exactly who needs that
difference spelled out rather than implied.

**Storage first, then bookkeeping**, and the order is load-bearing. A failed
object delete leaves everything untouched. A failed *record* afterwards means
the file is genuinely gone — so the UI says "the file was deleted, but its
queue record could not be settled" rather than "failed", which would imply the
file survived and invite a second attempt at something that no longer exists.

**Deletions go in `moderation_log`** alongside post moderation rather than a
second log nobody remembers to read, with the usage summary recorded as the
snapshot — the only record of what the deleter was told before deciding.

#### The storage policy, and a live hole it uncovered

Storage rules for this project lived in the Supabase dashboard rather than in
migrations — invisible to code review, and **absent from every preview
branch**, since preview databases are built from the migrations. Asking to see
them turned up something worse than the thing being asked about.

The bucket had four policies, all for `authenticated`:

```
Auth Upload      INSERT   bucket_id = 'wiki-media'
Auth Update      UPDATE   bucket_id = 'wiki-media'
Auth Delete      DELETE   bucket_id = 'wiki-media'
Auth List Media  SELECT   bucket_id = 'wiki-media'
```

**DELETE was open to any signed-in account.** Not staff — anyone who had
registered could have emptied the bucket, on a site about to be pointed at by a
1.4M-member Discord. Pre-existing, unrelated to this feature, and found only
because this feature made someone look.

**It also made the first draft of this migration useless.** It added a *second,
narrower* DELETE policy — and permissive policies are OR'd, so `Auth Delete`
would have gone on saying yes and `can_delete_media` would have shipped as pure
decoration: a checkbox in owner tools that controlled nothing while sitting
next to two that do.

So the existing policy is **replaced, not supplemented** — same name, so the
dashboard still shows one obvious DELETE rule instead of two that have to be
read together. And it is altered-if-present, created-if-absent, because
production has the policy and a preview branch does not: an unconditional
`ALTER` fails on the preview, an unconditional `CREATE` fails on production,
and neither half can be assumed.

Only DELETE is touched. INSERT and SELECT are how contributors upload and how
the editor lists the library, and both must stay open. **UPDATE is a separate
open question** — it permits renaming an object, which breaks every reference
to it — but nothing in the app uses it, so narrowing it belongs in its own
change rather than riding along.

Worth recording for next time: probing without a token got partway and no
further. Anon listing `wiki-media` returned `[]` despite 316 files, which
proved SELECT was scoped. The anon DELETE probe was **inconclusive** — storage
resolves the object before consulting the policy, so a missing file returns
`NoSuchKey` whatever the policy says. Reading the dashboard was the only way to
know, and the answer was not the one the encouraging probe suggested.

**Why this resolves the collector conflict:** the collector deletes only what nothing references, and this deletes what a person judged. They no longer race, because the queue's delete is manual and immediate rather than a rule the collector has to agree with.

### 6. Abuse resistance and the contributor leaderboard

From the plan's item 4, and the pieces that only become concrete once threads exist.

- [x] **Reporting.** Moderators removing posts they happen to see does not scale to 1.4M people. A report button is what turns moderation from patrolling into a queue — the same shape as the media queue already built

#### Shipped 2026-08-13

**There is deliberately no "incorrect" or "misinformation" category**, and this
is the one genuinely domain-specific decision in the feature. On a frame-data
wiki *"this is wrong"* is the most common complaint and the least actionable by
moderation — a thread is exactly where being wrong should be argued with.
Offering the category would fill the queue with *"this guy is wrong about
Sukuna's DP"* and train moderators to skim, which is how a real harassment
report gets missed. Reports are about **conduct**; corrections belong in a
reply or in a revision to the page.

The four that exist: spam, harassment, off topic, other.

**Anti-brigading, on the reports themselves.** A `UNIQUE` index on
`(target_id, reporter_id)` means twenty people organising against one post
produce twenty rows a moderator can see the shape of, while one person with a
script produces exactly one. The index cannot see somebody walking a thread
reporting *everything*, so a second limit caps it at 5 reports per 10 minutes.

**The reply is identical whether the report was new or a duplicate.** Telling
somebody "you already reported this" leaks that their earlier report exists and
has not been actioned, which invites them to try again from another account.

**Reporters cannot read their own reports back.** There is nothing in the row
they did not write, and a reporter who can watch the status learns exactly when
a moderator looked — useful for evading one.

**`report_count` is computed server-side** and shown as a badge. Three reports
on one post is a completely different situation from one, and it is the single
most useful signal in the queue; doing it client-side would be a query per row.

Acting on a report is **two writes in a deliberate order** — moderate the post,
then close the report. A failure between them leaves the report open over a
post already down, which is visible work to finish, rather than a closed report
over a post still on the page. The UI says "Post handled, but the report did
not close" rather than "failed", because "failed" would imply neither happened
and invite doing it twice.

Two harness bugs the tests caught, both of which meant a test was passing
without proving anything:

- `data-report-post` was on **both** the trigger button and the form, so the
  delegated click handler matched the form and re-opened it instead of
  submitting. The submit event never fired.
- The spec's `adminPrompt` stub used `window.__promptReply ?? 'a reason'`, and
  `null ?? x` returns `x` — so the "cancelling writes nothing" test was
  cancelling with a real reason and proving the opposite of its name.
- [x] **`viewer` must actually block posting.** The soft ban exists and means "signed in, can read, cannot submit". If threads do not honour it, the ban stops meaning anything the moment threads ship — enforced in the INSERT policy with `IS DISTINCT FROM`, never `<>`, because `get_my_role()` returns NULL for an ordinary contributor and `NULL <> 'viewer'` is NULL, which would have denied everyone
- [x] **Thread rate limiting is its own limit.** 20 seconds, in its own trigger
- [x] **Decide whether `bypass_cooldown` carries over.** **No** — owner's call, 2026-08-13. It was written for submissions, and the thread limit applies to everyone including admins, so no single compromised staff account can flood a page
- [ ] Contributor leaderboard

### 9. Bug fixes phase

The owner's list, 2026-08-13.

That is the same family as the v0.12 P0 in the merge compiler: not data destroyed outright, but a review process that quietly stops being a review. It belongs at the front of this phase.

Character states shipped in v0.12 and the admin preview was never taught about them — `js/admin-preview.js` builds the character page without the mode toggle `js/character_modes.js` injects, and `ultimateAtk` is a tab the preview does not know exists.

#### Fixed, 2026-08-13

Measured before building, against a revision that completely rewrites a skill
inside an ultimate state:

```
changedTabs: []      popup: none      marked tabs: none      state toggle: absent
```

No signal of any kind. The reviewer sees a page identical to the live one and
is told nothing — the whole bug in one line.

Four parts, in `js/admin-modes.js` and the three files that had to learn about
states:

- **A state toggle in the preview** (`#preview-mode-bar`), the same
  `.character-mode-chip` control the live page and the editor already use, so
  a reviewer picks a state with a control they know. Hidden for the pages that
  declare no states, which is most of them.
- **The Ultimate tab**, injected when either version has `ultimateAtk` moves.
  Deliberately unlike the live page, which also shows it empty for any
  registry-marked base-only character: a reviewer is not filling it in, and a
  permanently-empty tab in a review strip is one more place to check that never
  has anything in it.
- **Change detection narrowed to the state on screen.** `getTabData` compared
  an ultimate state's tabs against the base kit's, so a state edit read as a
  wholesale rewrite in one direction and as nothing at all in the other. Both
  sides now resolve through `resolveModeDesc` / `resolveModeFrame`.
- **A line in the popup naming the states that changed but are not on screen.**
  This is the part that actually closes the bug: markers can only describe the
  state being rendered, so something has to say "the edit is real, it is over
  there". The chips carry the same blue dot the tab strip uses.

Two things found on the way and fixed with it:

- **Intercept dropped the state.** `editCurrentTicket` appended `&tab=` but no
  `&mode=`, so a reviewer who switched to an ultimate state and intercepted
  landed on the base kit — and would have written their corrections into the
  wrong state. A mode-scoped ticket still overrides, being the stronger claim.
- **The preview reached into `modeData` directly** rather than through
  `resolveModeDesc`, losing the one documented exception: a state that has not
  overridden the portrait shows the character's. A state with no profile of its
  own rendered as a page with no character on it.

`tests/admin-preview-states.spec.js`, 8 tests. All 8 fail against the pre-fix
code.

Three more found by this project rather than reported, worth folding into the same phase:

- [ ] `cp.importance` interpolated unescaped into `innerHTML` (`js/description.js`, counterplay block). Same class as the matchup ones fixed in v0.13, left alone then to keep that PR to its two items
- [ ] ~40 `waitForTimeout` calls behind the v0.13 flake class. `retries: 2` made them survivable, not fixed
- [ ] Suite worker scaling: 4x workers buys 1.44x, and 8 is slower than 4. Supabase ruled out at 3%; a naive Node test server was worse. Still unexplained

#### Moderator access to the Overseer, 2026-08-13

The owner's correction to how moderation was shipped:

> *"It is fine to let the moderator into the overseer panel to moderate — it is
> THE staff page. But they can't see the submission queue and media queue,
> cause it is not their job. People with the moderate perk can see the button
> to navigate to the admin panel, there is no need to create a new moderator
> role."*

Still a capability and not a role, so `UNIQUE(user_id)` on `user_roles` is
untouched. `admin.html`'s gate now admits admin, reviewer **or** `can_moderate`;
a moderator-only account gets the report queue and nothing else.

**The two gates were changed together, on purpose.** `js/pagebuilder.js`
already carried a comment about the time they drifted — it listed roles
`admin.html` did not accept, so those users got a working-looking OVERSEER
button that dead-ended at ACCESS DENIED. `tests/moderator-access.spec.js` now
drives *both* real code paths against the same four inputs in one test, rather
than asserting them separately, because separate assertions are exactly what
let them drift last time. Verified by deliberately re-introducing the drift and
confirming the test fails.

**The queues are hidden, not removed.** Five files call `getElementById` on
those containers without guarding; deleting them would turn every one of those
into a null dereference for exactly one class of user — the one nobody tests by
hand. Hidden before anything loads, in JS rather than CSS, so no queue flashes
into view and disappears.

**This is presentation, not security.** `pending_revisions` and the media
tables have their own RLS and a moderator-only account cannot read them
whatever the page does. The claim is "not shown a job that is not theirs", not
"prevented from doing it" — worth stating, because a hidden div is not an
access control and should never be mistaken for one.

The report queue also loads on arrival for a moderator, rather than waiting for
a Load press: for them it is the entire page.

#### Fixed on the way: a latent race in `page-skeleton-parity`

Found while verifying reporting, and **confirmed pre-existing** — reproduced
3/3 on `next-update` with none of the reporting work applied, failing on a
different two or three characters each run.

`loadPageDescriptions` adds `vessel-content space-y-6` to `#tab-overview`,
`#tab-matchups` and `#tab-counterplay` once the page's data arrives. The
character boot fires it without awaiting, so whether it had run by the time the
spec snapshotted class lists depended on how fast a network response came back.
It asserted exact equality against the router's classes, so it lost that race
at random. Almost certainly latent for a long time and made reliable by the
suite growing — see the runtime measurements in §11, where CPU contention turns
out to stretch every navigation.

Ids and their order are still asserted exactly, and every router class is still
asserted present individually — including the load-bearing asymmetry where
`#tab-skills` gets `space-y-8` and `#tab-overview` gets plain `.tab-content`.
What is no longer asserted is the *absence* of classes the render layer
legitimately adds afterwards, which was never this spec's claim to make.

#### A local-only backend override, 2026-08-13

Owner's question, part-way through the tier list editor: *"Supabase database is
not updated with the new stuff yet. Do you think it is best if we make some
change for it to test things out, or we leave it to the release?"*

The honest state at that point: **five migrations, roughly eight RPCs and
fifteen policies, none of which had ever been executed by a real client.** Every
auth spec in this project mocks Supabase and never touches Postgres, so RLS
predicates, grant gaps, trigger ordering and RPC guards were all unverified.

**Branching proves a migration APPLIES. It does not prove the feature WORKS**,
and only the second claim matters on release day. Left alone, the first real
click on a release's worth of policies would have happened on production.

So `js/site_utils.js` can now point a locally served copy of the site at
another Supabase project — in practice a branching preview database. Guarded
twice, and both guards are deliberate:

1. **Hostname must be localhost or loopback.** `dogslamloop.com` cannot satisfy
   this whatever is in storage, and the function returns the production target
   *before ever reading storage* on a remote host.
2. **A localStorage key must be set by hand.** Visiting a local copy does not
   opt you in.

Neither guard depends on a build flag or an environment variable, because this
site has neither — it is static files served as-is, so the check has to be
something true of the running page.

A malformed override falls back to production rather than leaving the page with
no backend at all: a page with no client is worse than one on the wrong client,
because every feature silently does nothing and nothing says why. Only
`https://<ref>.supabase.co` is accepted, and an active override warns on every
single page load — testing against a preview while believing it is production,
or the reverse, is the one way this helps nobody.

`tests/supabase-override.spec.js`, 6 tests, most of them about the guards
rather than the feature. An override that could activate on the live site would
let anything able to write localStorage point real readers at somebody else's
database.

##### It drew blood on its first day, 2026-08-14

The owner created a preview branch, looked around, and **deleted it** — leaving
the override in localStorage pointing at a project that no longer existed.
Every request failed, login stopped working, and **nothing on screen said why**.

Two things were wrong, and only one of them was obvious.

**The console warning was useless.** It was written for somebody already
suspicious, and the entire failure mode of this feature is *not* being
suspicious of a page that looks completely normal. Replaced with an on-page
banner naming the target project and carrying a one-click **USE PRODUCTION**
button, so recovery needs no knowledge of the storage key or the console.

**`index.html` is the only page on the site with a CSP**, and its `connect-src`
pins the production project — so the override can never work there whatever the
client was built with. That was the wall of errors in the report. Deliberately
*not* fixed by widening the CSP: `connect-src` pinning is real defence in depth
on the most-visited page, and weakening production to enable a dev convenience
is the wrong trade. The banner names the problem on that page instead, and the
override works on every other page, which is all of the surface worth testing.

**The tests missed it because they tested configuration, not reachability.**
They asserted the client was *built with* the right URL — which was true, and
useless, since every request was then blocked or refused. A mock cannot notice
that a real network call would fail. The banner tests are behavioural instead:
the banner appears, says which project, warns on the page whose CSP blocks it,
and clears on click.

One of those tests failed first time for a harness reason worth recording:
`addInitScript` runs on **every** document load, so it re-wrote the override
during the reload and the banner could never be observed clearing itself. The
harness would have hidden the exact behaviour under test.

**Rejected: applying the migrations to production early.** It would work and
nothing would break — the tables sit unused until v0.14 ships — but production
would then match no git ref, which is the drift the whole migration pipeline
exists to prevent.

#### Suite runtime, measured 2026-08-13 — and the worker-scaling mystery, solved

Asked whether 710 tests is too many. Measured rather than guessed. Five
navigations of a real character page, idle machine:

| How the test navigates | Per navigation |
|---|---|
| `networkidle` + real CDNs — **what 311 of the 359 do** | **1474 ms** |
| `networkidle`, CDN scripts served locally | **768 ms** (−48%) |
| `domcontentloaded` + real CDNs | **301 ms** (−80%) |

Roughly one navigation per test, so ≈ 4.4 of the 6.9 minute run is *navigating*.
The assertions are nearly free. For contrast the 42 `waitForTimeout` calls
total **17.6 s** across the whole run — they are a flakiness problem, not a
speed problem, and the item above is about reliability rather than time.

**This explains §9's "still unexplained" worker scaling.** The first run of that
experiment happened while a full suite was saturating the CPU, and it showed
CDN-stubbing as *slower* — the opposite result. `networkidle` waits for 500 ms
of network silence, and under contention that silence takes longer to arrive,
so every navigation stretches. More workers → more contention → longer waits →
net loss. Supabase was ruled out back then at 3%; `networkidle` itself was
never the suspect.

**The count is not the problem and tests are not being deleted.** There is no
build step, no type checker and no staging environment, and a merge to `main`
deploys — the suite is the only thing between a typo and the live site.

- [ ] A global route fixture serving `supabase-js` from a local copy and
      dropping KaTeX in tests. One file, no test changes, roughly halves the
      run. Owner's call 2026-08-13: **do it in the fine-tuning phase**, not now
- [ ] Retire `networkidle` in favour of `domcontentloaded` plus an explicit
      wait for the thing the test actually cares about. The larger lever and it
      *reduces* flake, but it is ~300 call sites each needing judgement — some
      genuinely need data to have arrived. File by file, as they are touched

## Anti-brigading, for the record

The Free Submit list averages every vote, and an average has no defence against volume. That is the whole vulnerability.

**Brigading** is a coordinated group voting together to move a result. One message in a 1.4M-member Discord — *"everyone go rate this character S tier"* — produces hundreds of votes in minutes, and the average moves to wherever they pushed it. The same effect comes from one determined person with alt accounts.

It matters here specifically because the ranking would carry the wiki's official endorsement. A number that says "the community rates this S" is worth something only if it reflects the community rather than whoever organised best that afternoon.

The defences, roughly in order of value:

1. **One vote per account, enforced in the database.** A `UNIQUE` constraint, not a client check — resubmitting updates your vote instead of adding another.
2. **A gate on who may vote.** Account age, or at least one approved contribution. Alt accounts made for a single vote are the cheapest attack and this is what makes them expensive.
3. **Publish the sample size.** "Averaged from 412 votes" lets a reader judge for themselves. A ranking with no vote count cannot be evaluated at all, which is why the plan called it meaningless.
4. **Median rather than a plain average** — chosen, 2026-08-13. A coordinated block of extreme votes drags a mean and barely moves a median. The single most effective change for the least work, and free at this stage because nothing is built yet.
5. **Show the distribution.** If opinion is genuinely split, a bar chart says so; an average silently reports the midpoint as consensus, which is a different claim entirely.
6. **Rate limiting**, so a script cannot submit faster than people can.

None of these stop a determined brigade outright. Together they make it expensive, visible, and much less rewarding — and 3 and 5 mean that even a successful brigade is legible to anyone looking, rather than laundered into a clean-looking number.
